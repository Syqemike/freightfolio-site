Do I Need a Website If My Business Is on Facebook?

Facebook is free, so why pay for a website? An honest look at what Facebook does well, where it falls short, and why most local businesses need both.

It is a fair question, and one we hear a lot from Mississippi Gulf Coast business owners: "My customers find me on Facebook. Why would I pay for a website?" For some businesses, a Facebook page genuinely covers the basics. But there are limits to running your entire online presence on rented land. Here is an honest look at both sides.

What Facebook does well

Facebook is free, your customers are already there, and for many local businesses it works as a lightweight storefront. You can post updates, share photos, collect reviews, answer messages, and run cheap local ads. If someone searches your business name on Facebook, they find your hours, your photos, and a way to contact you. For a brand-new business with zero budget, that is a legitimate starting point.

Where Facebook falls short

You do not own it. Facebook can change what your page looks like, how many followers see your posts, or the rules for business pages at any time. Organic reach on business page posts has been shrinking for years: most of your followers will never see most of what you post unless you pay to boost it.

Google barely sees it. When someone searches "plumber near me" or "best bakery in Biloxi," Google shows websites and Google Business Profiles, not Facebook pages, in the top results. A Facebook-only business is nearly invisible to the large share of customers who start on Google.

It caps your credibility. Customers notice the difference between a business with its own website and one with only a Facebook page, especially for higher-ticket services. A website signals that the business is established and invested. A Facebook-only presence can read as temporary or a side project, fair or not.

You cannot control the experience. Your page sits inside Facebook's layout, surrounded by ads, suggested competitors, and distractions. You cannot control the design, the flow from "interested" to "contact me," or what a visitor sees first.

What a website gives you that Facebook cannot

A website is an asset you control. Your domain is yours, your design serves your business instead of Facebook's ad model, and every visitor is there for you, not scrolling past you. A site lets you show up in Google searches, tell your full story with clear structure (services, pricing, galleries, testimonials), and capture leads through contact forms instead of hoping someone sends a Messenger message you might miss.

It also compounds. A good website keeps working while you sleep, ranks for more searches over time, and makes every other marketing effort, including your Facebook page, more effective because there is somewhere credible to send people.

The practical answer: use both, with the website as home base

The businesses that do best online treat their website as home base and Facebook as an outpost: post and engage on Facebook, but link everything back to the site where you control the experience and capture the lead.

Consider a hypothetical example. A barbershop in Ocean Springs posts haircut photos on Facebook, where regulars like and share them. But when someone new moves to town and Googles "barbershop Ocean Springs," they find the shop's website: services, prices, online booking, and reviews, all in one clean page. Facebook brought the social proof; the website closed the customer. Neither could do the other's job.

Common objections, answered

"My customers are all on Facebook." Maybe today. But new customers, the ones who just moved to town or just realized they need what you sell, start on Google far more often than on Facebook. A Facebook page captures demand from people who already know you; a website captures demand from people who do not know you yet. You need both kinds.

"I get enough business by word of mouth." Word of mouth still ends online. When someone recommends you, the next step is almost always a search: the potential customer Googles your business name to check you out. What they find in that moment, a solid website or a thin Facebook page, shapes whether they call. Your referral network deserves a good landing spot.

"A website is too expensive for what I need." That was more true ten years ago. Between DIY builders and done-for-you subscriptions starting around $149 a month with no upfront cost, the price of a basic professional site is now in the same range as many businesses' monthly ad spend. Compare it against one lost customer, not against zero.

"I tried a website once and it did nothing." A site that nobody can find does nothing. Most disappointing websites failed at the basics: they were never set up to appear in local searches, had no clear contact path, or went stale within months. Those are fixable problems, not proof that websites do not work.
