How Much Does a Website Cost for a Small Business in Mississippi?

What does a website cost for a Mississippi small business? Honest breakdown of upfront builds, DIY builders, and $149-$299/mo done-for-you plans.

If you run a small business in Mississippi and you have ever priced out a website, you have probably seen numbers all over the map: a nephew who will do it for a few hundred bucks, an agency quoting five figures, and a dozen ads for do-it-yourself builders at $25 a month. It is hard to know what a website should actually cost, and harder to know what you are really paying for.

This article breaks down what drives website cost for a small local business, the three common ways businesses pay for sites, and how to figure out which option fits your situation. No sales pitch disguised as advice: just the honest math.

The three ways small businesses pay for websites

Almost every option falls into one of three buckets.

1. Pay upfront for a custom build. A freelancer or agency designs and builds your site, you pay once, and the site is yours. For a small business site, this commonly runs from a few thousand dollars into the low five figures depending on size and features. After launch, you still pay separately for hosting, domain renewals, and any future changes, which are usually billed hourly.

2. Build it yourself with a website builder. Tools like Wix, Squarespace, or Weebly charge roughly $20 to $50 a month. The cash cost is low, but the hidden cost is your time: learning the tool, writing the copy, finding photos, and fixing things when they break. For a busy owner, that time has a price too.

3. A done-for-you subscription. You pay a flat monthly rate and someone else designs, builds, hosts, and manages the site. This is the model Freightfolio uses: plans run $149 to $299 a month with $0 upfront, and the monthly price covers design, hosting, and ongoing management. Cancel anytime and the site comes down, since you are renting the service rather than buying the asset.

What actually drives the cost

Whether you pay upfront or monthly, the same factors move the price:

Number of pages. A simple 3-page site (home, services, contact) costs far less to build and maintain than a 12-page site with galleries, team bios, and service-area pages.

Features. Online booking, contact forms, photo galleries, and review integrations each add build time. A basic informational site is the cheapest thing to run.

Copy and photos. Someone has to write the words and supply the images. If you provide them, it costs less. If the designer sources or writes them, expect to pay for that work.

Ongoing changes. Businesses change hours, add services, and run seasonal promotions. A site that never gets updated goes stale fast, so ask what updates cost after launch before you commit to anything.

The hidden costs people forget to budget

The sticker price is rarely the whole price. Ask about domain registration and renewals, hosting fees, SSL certificates, and what happens when you want a change six months from now. An upfront build that looks cheap can get expensive once you add a year of hosting and a few rounds of paid updates.

With a subscription model, those line items are the point: hosting, management, and a set number of monthly updates are baked into the flat rate, so there are no surprise invoices. The tradeoff is that you do not own the site outright. If you cancel, it comes down after a grace period. For many small businesses that is a fair trade; for some, ownership matters more.

How to decide what is right for your business

Start with two questions. First, how much is your time worth? If a DIY builder saves you $100 a month but costs you ten hours you could spend on paying work, it is not actually cheaper. Second, how often will the site need to change? A business that updates menus, services, or photos regularly gets more value from a managed plan than one whose site can sit untouched for years.

As a rough guide: if you have more time than money and enjoy tinkering, a DIY builder is reasonable. If you want a professional site but cannot justify several thousand dollars upfront, a subscription spreads the cost into something closer to a utility bill. If you want full ownership and have the budget, an upfront custom build makes sense, but budget for the ongoing costs too.

Whatever you choose, get the full picture in writing before you pay: what is included, what updates cost, who owns what, and how you get out. A reputable provider will answer all four without hedging. You can see exactly how we answer them on our pricing and FAQ pages.

A note on "free" websites

You will also see offers for free websites. Read the fine print: free builders typically put their own branding and ads on your site, give you a subdomain instead of your own domain name (yourbusiness.freebuilder.com), and charge you the moment you want the ads removed or a proper domain connected. By the time a free site looks professional, it usually costs as much as a paid builder plan, and you have still done all the work yourself.

The same skepticism applies to "free website with hosting purchase" bundles. The website is rarely free; the cost is baked into an inflated hosting plan with a steep renewal rate in year two. Always price the second year, not the intro offer alone.

What good value looks like

Price alone does not tell you whether a deal is good. A $149-a-month plan that includes design, hosting, management, and regular updates can be better value than a $3,000 upfront build that charges $150 an hour for every change afterward. When comparing options, add up the realistic two-year cost: upfront fees plus hosting plus the updates you will actually need. That is the number that matters.

Also weigh speed. A DIY site that takes you three months of evenings to finish is three months without the customers it would have brought in. Sometimes the cheapest option is the one that gets you live fastest.
